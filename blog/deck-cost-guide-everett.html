Decks & Patios Cost in Everett, WA (2026 Guide)

Published July 2026 by NW General Contractor

For homeowners researching decks & patios cost Everett, a practical 2026 planning range is typically $8,000–$50,000, depending on whether the project is a simple patio, new deck, major rebuild, or covered outdoor space. Materials, site access, structural work, finishes, permits, and labor can move an Everett estimate below or above that range.

What a decks & patios costs in Everett

The average cost of a decks & patios in Everett is difficult to reduce to one number because decks and patios are built differently. A deck is an elevated structure that may require footings, framing, stairs, railings, and structural connections. A patio is usually constructed at or near grade using concrete, pavers, gravel, or another hardscape material. Those differences affect labor, materials, permits, and timeline.

For early budgeting, an uncomplicated ground-level patio may typically fall around $8,000–$20,000, while a straightforward new deck may typically cost around $15,000–$40,000. Larger decks, elevated structures, premium decking, extensive stairs, covered areas, or combined deck-and-patio projects may commonly reach approximately $40,000–$100,000, depending on scope. These are planning ranges rather than quotes; site conditions and design decisions can change the actual proposal substantially.

Homeowners comparing decks & patios price per square foot Everett figures should use them cautiously. A basic patio may sometimes budget around $20–$50 per square foot, while a conventionally framed deck may often budget around $45–$100 per square foot. Complex or premium deck construction may fall around $80–$175 per square foot. Small projects can have a higher unit price because mobilization, demolition, permitting, equipment, and finish work are spread across less area.

Everett properties also vary in grade, drainage, access, and existing construction. A flat backyard with direct equipment access is usually easier to price than a sloped or confined site. Before choosing a budget from an online calculator, have the contractor identify what the price includes: demolition, disposal, excavation, footings, framing, surface materials, railings, stairs, drainage work, permits, finishes, and cleanup.

What drives the price

Project scope has the greatest overall influence on cost. Resurfacing a sound deck is a different project from replacing deteriorated framing, and an uncovered patio is different from a roofed outdoor living area. Size matters, but structural height, shape, stairs, guardrails, integrated seating, privacy screens, lighting preparation, and transitions to doors or walkways can matter just as much.

Labor increases when crews must remove old construction, hand-carry materials through narrow side yards, work on a slope, build high above grade, or make careful connections to an existing house. Hidden deterioration can also expand the scope after demolition. A responsible estimate should state how concealed damage will be documented, priced, and approved if it is discovered.

Materials create another large range. Pressure-treated framing and economical surface products generally cost less than premium composite decking, specialty hardwood, metal railing systems, porcelain pavers, or elaborate masonry. Material price alone does not tell the whole story: some products require additional framing, specialized fasteners, more detailed installation, or manufacturer-specific handling.

Finishes affect both appearance and maintenance. Wood decks may need cleaning, staining, or sealing, while concrete patios may include color, texture, scoring, or protective treatment. Paver patios need a properly prepared base and edge restraint. The least expensive finish at installation may not be the least expensive choice over the outdoor space's service life.

Permits and related documentation may be needed depending on the structure, location, height, attachments, electrical work, and other project details. Permit fees and preparation can add roughly $500–$3,000 to some projects, while more involved plans or professional design services may increase that planning allowance to approximately $2,000–$8,000. The applicable requirements should be confirmed for the specific Everett property rather than assumed from a neighboring project.

  • Overall size, elevation, shape, and structural complexity
  • Decking, concrete, pavers, railings, fasteners, and finish selections
  • Demolition, disposal, excavation, drainage, and site preparation
  • Backyard access, slope, soil conditions, and material-handling distance
  • Stairs, built-in seating, privacy features, lighting preparation, and covered areas
  • Permit requirements, plans, inspections, and coordination with related trades

Cost ranges by project scope

A repair or maintenance project may typically cost around $2,000–$10,000 when the work involves cleaning, refinishing, replacing limited deck boards, or correcting a contained problem. That range assumes the primary structure can remain. If inspection reveals widespread deterioration or unsafe framing, partial repair may no longer be the appropriate scope.

A basic patio may typically fall around $8,000–$20,000 for a modest area with relatively direct access and limited site preparation. Concrete finish choices, excavation, drainage, retaining needs, paver patterns, and difficult access can push the project toward approximately $18,000–$35,000. Outdoor kitchens, substantial covers, fire features, or extensive utility work would require separate allowances and trade-specific evaluation.

A small or straightforward deck may typically range from $15,000–$30,000. A medium deck with stairs, railings, upgraded surface materials, or more demanding framing may commonly range from $25,000–$55,000. An elevated, multilevel, premium, or covered deck may fall around $50,000–$100,000, depending on engineering needs, access, structural details, and finishes.

A combined outdoor-living project involving both a deck and patio may typically budget around $35,000–$100,000. The advantage of planning them together is that elevations, stairs, drainage, pathways, and material transitions can be coordinated. The estimate should still separate major components so homeowners can see where the budget is going and evaluate optional features.

Construction time also varies by scope. A straightforward patio may usually take about 1–3 weeks after approvals and material availability. A conventional deck may usually require about 2–6 weeks. A larger, covered, or highly detailed outdoor-living project may take approximately 6–14 weeks. Weather, inspections, demolition findings, product lead times, and scope changes can extend those ranges.

  • Limited repair or refinishing: typically $2,000–$10,000
  • Basic patio: typically $8,000–$20,000
  • Upgraded or site-intensive patio: typically $18,000–$35,000
  • Small or straightforward deck: typically $15,000–$30,000
  • Medium deck with stairs or upgrades: typically $25,000–$55,000
  • Complex, elevated, premium, or covered deck: typically $50,000–$100,000
  • Combined deck-and-patio project: typically $35,000–$100,000

How to get an accurate estimate

An accurate estimate starts with a defined scope and an on-site review. Measure the approximate area, collect inspiration photos, identify preferred materials, and decide which features are essential. Tell the contractor whether the goal is a new installation, complete replacement, resurfacing, repair, or a coordinated deck-and-patio design. Photos help communicate style, but they cannot reveal soil, framing, drainage, or access conditions.

For an existing deck, the contractor should evaluate the visible framing, surface condition, stairs, railings, supports, and connections that can be accessed. For a patio, the review should consider grade, runoff, excavation, base preparation, nearby structures, and the route crews will use to move material. Concealed conditions should be identified as an estimate limitation rather than silently assumed.

Ask for a written proposal that distinguishes included work from allowances and exclusions. It should identify demolition, disposal, structural work, surface materials, railing, stairs, finishes, site restoration, permit responsibility, and cleanup where applicable. Product names or clearly described quality levels make competing bids easier to compare than vague phrases such as standard material.

The proposed timeline should separate planning and approvals from active construction. Depending on project complexity and agency workload, design, selections, and permitting may add roughly 2–10 weeks before construction. Material ordering may overlap with part of that period, but specialty products can have longer lead times. A realistic schedule should also allow for Everett-area weather and inspection sequencing.

Smart ways to save without cutting corners

The most reliable savings come from simplifying the design before construction begins. Rectangular layouts, standard material dimensions, direct stair runs, and fewer level changes can reduce labor and waste while still producing a useful outdoor space. Keeping a patio near grade is often less structurally demanding than creating a large elevated deck, although site drainage and access still need proper consideration.

Spend where durability and safety depend on it: preparation, drainage, footings, framing, fastening, flashing, and suitable materials. Decorative upgrades are easier to defer than structural corrections. If the budget is tight, ask the contractor to separate core construction from optional items such as elaborate patterns, premium railings, built-in seating, privacy features, or a future cover.

Compare lifecycle needs as well as installation price. A lower-cost wood surface may fit the initial budget but can involve recurring cleaning and refinishing. A higher-priced composite option may reduce some maintenance, but product lines and installation requirements vary. Request at least two material packages priced for the same layout so the comparison remains meaningful.

Planning early can also limit expensive changes. Finalize dimensions, door transitions, stair locations, furniture zones, and finish selections before ordering materials. If a future hot tub, cover, outdoor kitchen, or other heavy feature is being considered, disclose it during design so the structure and utilities can be evaluated for that intended use.

Finally, keep a contingency rather than using the entire budget on visible finishes. For renovation work, a contingency of roughly 10%–20% of the estimated project range may be prudent, depending on how much existing construction is concealed. That allowance is not automatically spent; it provides room if demolition reveals deterioration, drainage problems, or other conditions that could not be confirmed beforehand.

  • Use a simple footprint and standard material dimensions
  • Price comparable basic and upgraded material packages
  • Preserve sound existing components only after they are evaluated
  • Defer optional decorative features instead of reducing structural scope
  • Finalize selections before ordering and construction
  • Plan a roughly 10%–20% contingency, depending on uncertainty
