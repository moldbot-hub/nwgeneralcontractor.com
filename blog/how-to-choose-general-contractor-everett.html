How to Choose a General Contractor in Everett: Verification and Scope

Everything you need to know to hire a trustworthy contractor for your home improvement project

By NW General Contractor. Updated October 11, 2026 after a substantive scope and source review.

Start with the business you will actually contract with

Match the proposal’s legal business name and registration number to the contractor’s record. Washington L&I hiring guidance recommends checking status, bond and insurance information, reviewing history, obtaining references and comparing at least three written bids. Registration is an important check, but it does not guarantee workmanship or prevent disputes.

Verify registration and the right trade credentials

Use L&I’s verification service before signing and check again if the project is delayed. Confirm which business is responsible for the work and which trades need separate licensing or certification. A general contractor registration should not be treated as proof of every specialist qualification. Ask about subcontractors and who coordinates their work.

Understand the bond distinction

L&I’s contractor registration guidance lists a $30,000 bond for general contractors and a $15,000 bond for specialty contractors. Check the actual record and current requirements. A registration bond has limits and claim requirements; it is not a promise that every loss will be reimbursed or that your entire project is insured.

Ask for current insurance information and clarify workers’ compensation arrangements where applicable. Read the coverage rather than assuming that a marketing phrase establishes the amount or scope. For a dispute, lien question or claim deadline, consult L&I’s current resources and qualified legal advice for the particular facts.

Compare written scopes before totals

  • Work included and excluded, with dimensions and product selections.
  • Allowances and owner-supplied items, including installation responsibilities.
  • Design, permits, trade coordination and inspections.
  • Sequence, milestones, known dependencies and occupancy assumptions.
  • Payment terms and what must happen before each payment.
  • Change authorization, cleanup, completion criteria and warranty terms.

A lump-sum contract can still have a detailed scope. A lower price is a reason to ask about differences, not proof of dishonesty. Normalize the scope and identify unresolved assumptions before choosing a bid.

Ask references about a comparable project

Request recent relevant examples that the contractor has permission to share. Ask the owner what changed during work, how extra costs were approved, how communication worked and whether closeout issues were resolved. Distinguish actual completed work from inspirational or illustrative images. Reviews can help, but a rating or lack of online reviews is not conclusive evidence of quality.

Read payments and changes carefully

Put the payment schedule, deposits, special-order materials, cancellation provisions and change process in writing. This guide does not impose a universal legal deposit percentage. Ask what each payment purchases and what documentation you receive. Do not accept pressure to sign terms you do not understand; consider independent advice for a substantial or unusual contract.

Questions for the estimate visit

  • Who is the day-to-day contact and how will updates be recorded?
  • What can be confirmed now, and what requires investigation?
  • How are occupied rooms, belongings and access protected?
  • Who submits approvals and responds to corrections?
  • How are substitutions, unexpected conditions and additional work authorized?
  • Which warranty applies, and how do I request service?
